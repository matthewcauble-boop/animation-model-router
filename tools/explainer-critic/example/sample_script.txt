# Why bread goes stale (sample script, about 75 seconds)

A loaf of bread can go stale in two days, and it has barely lost any water.

That sounds wrong. Stale bread feels dry, so surely it dried out? But weigh a fresh slice and a stale one, and they're almost the same.

So what actually changed?

Inside fresh bread, starch sits in a soft, messy tangle, like cooked spaghetti that has just come out of the pot. Baking blew those starch chains apart and soaked them in water.

As the loaf cools, the chains start lining up again, packing into tight little crystals. Think of a crowd that slowly forms neat rows. The water is still there, but it's locked inside those rows instead of keeping the crumb soft.

Here's the catch: the fridge makes it worse. Starch crystals form fastest just above freezing, so a loaf in the fridge goes stale about three times faster than one on the counter.

And the freezer does the opposite. At minus eighteen degrees the chains are frozen in place, so nothing lines up.

Which means stale bread isn't ruined. A few minutes in a hot oven shakes the crystals loose again, and the crumb goes soft.

So next time a loaf feels tired, don't bin it. Toast it, and you're undoing chemistry.
